On optimization and why the gradient is your only friend in the dark

2026-03-08

When you don't know the shape of the loss surface — and you never really do — the gradient $\nabla f(x)$ is the single piece of local information you can trust. Everything else is extrapolation.

The black-box model forces this. An oracle tells you $f(x)$ and $\nabla f(x)$ at a point. That's it. No global picture. No curvature unless you pay for it with a second-order oracle and Hessian computation. The pessimist reads this as a limitation; the analyst reads it as a classification: the problem now belongs to a class, and the class has known worst-case bounds.

Convergence in the convex case

For $L$-smooth convex $f$ with gradient descent step $\eta = 1/L$, the standard analysis gives:

$$f(x_k) - f^* \;\leq\; \frac{L \|x_0 - x^*\|^2}{2k}$$

So $O(1/\varepsilon)$ iterations for $\varepsilon$-accuracy — each iteration costs one gradient evaluation. The bound is tight on quadratics. Nesterov's accelerated method achieves $O(1/\sqrt{\varepsilon})$, which is optimal for this class.

What I find striking is how the step size $\eta = 1/L$ falls directly out of minimizing the quadratic upper bound — not from some ad-hoc schedule but from the structure of the problem class itself.

When the gradient fails

The moment $f$ is non-differentiable — say $f(x) = \max_i f_i(x)$ — the subgradient replaces the gradient, and convergence slows to $O(1/\sqrt{k})$ even with perfect step schedule. You can't escape this; it's a lower bound too.

Mirror descent extends the geometry: rather than the Euclidean prox step, you pick a Bregman divergence matched to your constraint set. On the simplex, KL divergence is natural, and the exponential gradient update follows. The algorithm is the same shape; only the measuring stick changes.
